## Proposed narration — not approved

The recipe can stay the same. The chocolate doesn’t have to. Chocolate contains cocoa butter, a fat that forms crystals. Melt it enough, and that crystal structure breaks down. As it cools, crystals form again. But their arrangements, and the network they build, can differ. The result can be softer chocolate with less snap. Tempering guides that rebuilding with controlled temperatures and mixing. Existing crystals can also help seed it. The goal is a structure that helps chocolate snap cleanly and look glossy. ## Failure modes and terminology

Poor temper: inappropriate crystallization/network, potentially softness/poor fracture/dullness; no single symptom diagnoses a polymorph. Fat bloom: surface fat redistribution/crystallization, multiple routes; V→VI not universal. Sugar bloom: moisture dissolves sugar and drying leaves sugar crystals. Both may coexist. Small accidental water/steam can cause grainy seizure while melted; distinct from bad temper, and sufficient intentional liquid is different. Overheating/scorching is a separate possible damage route, with exact molecular mechanism/threshold not established here. Composition and storage cycling change outcomes; milk/white are not the same recipe as dark. Graininess, crumbliness and streaks cannot all be assigned one cause. No sample-safety inference from whiteness. Bloom/seizure/scorching stay outside the concise narration.

Polymorph means different crystalline molecular arrangements; network describes their larger organization. Common I–VI convention maps V to β2 and VI to β1 in cited literature; pure TAG naming differs. V is generally sought and metastable, VI more thermodynamically stable; neither means one universal correct crystal or guaranteed quality. Nucleation initiates crystals; growth/aggregation build structure. Snap relates to stiffness/fracture, not sound alone. Gloss depends partly on surface smoothness/light scattering, not simply a form label. Sufficient melting removes structure; partial melting may preserve seeds. Commercial equipment manages heat exchange/agitation; suitable seeds can guide growth. Proper setting/contraction helps mold release, with mold/cooling caveats.

Quantities stay in research: six conventional forms (debated definitions); reported V 30–34.5°C / VI 33.5–38.5°C from a review compilation, not universal sample constants. Purdue dark-without-milkfat example 40–45→28–29→31–32°C is an illustrative process, not all chocolate. Other sources use different melting stages. No universal dark/milk/white temperature chart asserted. No exact numbers in narration.
