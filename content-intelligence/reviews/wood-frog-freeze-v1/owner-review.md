## wood-frog-freeze-tolerance.claim.freeze-survival

**CLAIM**

Wood frogs can survive nonlethal freezing in which up to about two-thirds of total body water becomes extracellular ice.

**STATUS**

REVISE · current package state: SUPPORTED · recommended after owner review: VERIFIED

**WHY**

The revised wording states the approximate amount and, crucially, that survivable ice is extracellular rather than uniform inside cells.

**SOURCE(S) / EVIDENCE LOCATION / IMPORTANT EVIDENCE**

- **Hibernation physiology, freezing adaptation and extreme freeze tolerance in a northern population of the wood frog** — Introduction, paragraph 2; Discussion, Freeze tolerance, paragraphs 3–5
  The article describes survival with up to two-thirds of body water frozen and treats 67% body ice as an approximate lethal threshold, while emphasizing population variation.
- **Molecular Physiology of Freeze Tolerance in Vertebrates** — Section II.B, freeze-tolerant frogs; discussion of extracellular ice and 65–70% total-body-water conversion
  The review synthesizes experimental literature describing 65–70% of total body water as extracellular ice in freeze-tolerant frogs.

**LIMITATIONS / CAVEATS**

- The two-thirds figure is approximate and represents an upper range, not every freeze.

**RECOMMENDED FINAL WORDING**

Wood frogs can survive nonlethal freezing in which up to about two-thirds of total body water becomes extracellular ice.

# Evidence-grounded script

A wood frog can survive a freeze that stops its heartbeat—then thaw and recover.

Much of its body water can turn to ice, mainly outside its cells.

As extracellular ice grows, water leaves the cells. That dehydration helps keep dangerous ice crystals from forming inside them.

Its liver rapidly releases glucose, while urea has already built up before freezing.

These cryoprotectants limit ice formation and help protect cells through freezing and thawing.

As the frog thaws, its heart starts beating first; breathing and leg reflexes follow. It survived by controlling the freeze—not by staying unfrozen.

# Narrative structure

- **Reversible cardiac arrest:** Create immediate disbelief without implying death or resurrection.
- **Extracellular freeze:** Establish substantial body-water freezing without depicting uniform intracellular ice.
- **Water leaves the cells:** Show how extracellular ice, dehydration and extra-organ sequestration reduce dangerous ice inside tissues and cells.
- **Two defenses, different timing:** Distinguish freeze-triggered glucose mobilization from urea accumulated before freezing.
- **Ordered recovery:** Show heart function returning before breathing and hindleg reflexes, then land the controlled-freeze distinction.
