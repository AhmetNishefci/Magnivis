## Exact proposed hook and narration

How can trying to keep your balance make a bridge sway more?

How can trying to keep your balance make a bridge sway more? On opening day, London’s Millennium Bridge moved sideways. When a walkway shifts, people adjust where they step to stay upright. In a crowd, those sideways forces can feed energy back into the bridge. Instead of dying away, the sway can grow. That can begin without everyone matching footsteps. Later coordination may change the motion; its exact role that day remains uncertain. Engineers added dampers to take energy out of the movement, suppressing the sway.

All factual segments are bound to selected claims. Text matches the historical owner handoff byte-for-byte in UTF-8; this does not demonstrate identity with any lost file or media.

## Exclusions and uncertainty

- exclude: “Everyone deliberately walked in step.” No evidence for deliberate crowd-wide lockstep.
- reject: “Crowd-wide synchronization was necessarily the sole initiating cause.” Balance models permit instability without that necessary condition.
- reject: “Synchronization played no role at all.” Later coherence can influence response; event attribution is limited.
- reject: “The primary problem was vertical bouncing.” Contemporary investigation describes lateral motion.
- exclude: “The bridge simply exceeded its weight capacity.” Dynamic instability is not evidence of exceeding static weight capacity.
- reject: “Dampers made future motion physically impossible.” Damping dissipates energy; it does not prohibit displacement.
- exclude: “The problem could never recur.” No absolute future guarantee is established.
- exclude-unknown: “No one was injured.” No sufficiently authoritative inspected evidence establishing injury status.
