# AHMET — CYCLE #14 PREMISE REVIEW

**Recommendation: The dice with no best choice.**

**PLANNED VIDEO / CENTRAL CURIOSITY:** With a special set of four numbered dice, whichever one you choose, another has a two-thirds chance of beating it. How can every choice have a better opponent when the dice themselves are fair?

**WHY A COLD VIEWER MAY CARE:** Choosing first normally feels like an advantage. Here you can inspect the numbers, make your choice, and still face a stronger reply. The viewer has a concrete puzzle to resolve without already caring about mathematics. The appeal survives without animation or dramatic copy; it is not merely that the dice have unusual numbers.

**EXPECTED PAYOFF:** Understand how the chances arise from the faces, and why “more likely to win” need not create a best-to-worst ranking. The advantages form a circle, like rock-paper-scissors, even though every individual roll compares ordinary numbers. The important distinction is between the result of one roll and the relationship between two distributions. This is a specific mathematical example, not a universal claim about rankings.

**POTENTIAL OPENING:** “Choose any of these four dice. Whichever you pick, another has better odds against it.” Show the unusual numbered faces prominently and establish that the higher roll wins. This is a proposed hook, not an approved script. A visually led pairing is also plausible; no mandatory question opening, palette, runtime, caption treatment or CTA is selected.

**INTENDED STORY:**

1. Present the four dice and the simple rule; invite the viewer to anticipate the strongest choice.
2. Reveal a favorable reply and show why it wins more often. Start with the simplest pairing: a die showing 3 on every face beats one with four 2s and two 6s in four of six equally likely cases.
3. Carry the same objects into the next comparisons. Each apparent winner has a counter; the accumulating evidence closes into a circle.
4. Return to the original choice: selecting second lets you choose a favorable match-up. It gives a probability advantage, not certainty.

The explanation should unfold as a demonstration. It must not ask the viewer to memorize four face tables or sit through four repetitive calculation sequences. The ending earns the insight by making the original choice understandable; no spoken engagement outro is needed by default.

**VISUAL OPPORTUNITIES:** Large, inspectable dice with material presence; turning a die to reveal repeated faces; expanding a simple match-up into equally likely outcomes; changing opponents in the same spatial relationship; a final wider view revealing the closed chain. Movement can carry the proof rather than decorate labels. Original spatial illustration or another accurate object treatment could serve this well. These are feasibility concepts only. No generated imagery, footage acquisition, audio, scene authoring or render occurred.

[Harvard Mathematics](https://www.math.harvard.edu/event/nontransitive-and-balanced-sets-of-dice/) independently describes the four-die Efron cycle with two-thirds pairwise advantages. A small feasibility enumeration of all 36 equally likely face pairs confirms 24 wins and no ties in each neighboring match-up. This is preliminary source inspection and arithmetic, not completed research development or final claim verification. Microsoft is an institutional explainer; Harvard is a seminar abstract, not a full proof. No physical fairness test or played game is claimed.

**LIMITATIONS:** These are specially numbered dice, not four ordinary 1–6 dice. “Fair” means each face is equally likely. The two-thirds result assumes independent one-die rolls and applies to the specified favorable pairing. It does not mean winning every roll, exactly six of nine rolls, or the same advantage under different rules. No Buffett/Gates anecdote or gambling advice is proposed. The main editorial risk is bookkeeping: if a cold viewer cannot follow the visible comparisons, this becomes a probability lecture. Approval would permit development to test comprehension; it would not obligate production if the promise fails.
