# MAGNIVIS — CYCLE #23: HE SURVIVED TWO ATOMIC BOMBS

## Mission

Research and produce a premium cinematic V1 master candidate about Tsutomu Yamaguchi, the Japanese engineer who survived both the Hiroshima and Nagasaki atomic bombings in August 1945.

Working title: **He Survived Two Atomic Bombs**

Category: Extraordinary true history / World War II / Human survival

Target runtime: 50–65 seconds, allowing the narrative to breathe.

## 2. Historical research

Establish a claim-level, independently verified chronology.

Investigate:

- Yamaguchi's employment and reason for being in Hiroshima.
- His location and circumstances during the August 6 bombing.
- His injuries and immediate survival.
- His journey back to Nagasaki.
- His whereabouts and activities when the second bomb detonated on August 9.
- The documented effects on him and his family.
- His later life, recognition and death in 2010.
- The distinction between being officially recognized as a double survivor and being the only person to survive both bombings.

Prioritize Hiroshima and Nagasaki memorial institutions, reputable historical archives, credible biographies, contemporary reporting and primary testimony where accessible.

Distinguish Yamaguchi's own recollections from independently corroborated details.

Do not invent dialogue, exact distances, personal thoughts or undocumented events.

Avoid presenting the bombings primarily as a spectacle. Acknowledge their catastrophic human consequences with historical dignity.

## 3. Storytelling direction

### Opening — 0–5 seconds

Begin with an intimate, cinematic portrait of a Japanese engineer in 1945.

Suggested hook:

"He survived an atomic bomb. Three days later, he survived another."

Immediately establish that this is a real, documented story.

### Hiroshima — 5–20 seconds

Show Yamaguchi in Hiroshima during a business trip.

Build toward August 6, 1945.

Represent the bombing through restrained, historically grounded visual storytelling: light, distance, environmental impact and aftermath rather than graphic injury or sensational destruction.

Explain that he survived despite being injured.

### The journey — 20–34 seconds

Show his return from Hiroshima to Nagasaki.

This should be a major visual transition.

Use an elegant historical map, train journey and change of city to communicate the extraordinary sequence.

Avoid invented travel details.

### Nagasaki — 34–49 seconds

Establish that Yamaguchi was back in Nagasaki on August 9.

Then reveal that the second atomic bomb struck.

This is the emotional and narrative climax.

Avoid presenting survival as immunity to radiation or implying that he was unharmed.

### The aftermath — 49–65 seconds

Explain that he survived both bombings and lived until 2010, aged 93.

Mention his official recognition with precise wording.

End with a memorable but respectful observation about the extraordinary events he witnessed and survived.

Do not claim he was the only double survivor.

## 4. Creative direction

The video must feel like a short historical film, not a slideshow.

Prioritize:

- Cinematic 1940s Japanese urban environments.
- A consistent, historically plausible representation of Yamaguchi.
- Strong spatial and chronological continuity.
- Atmospheric lighting and meaningful camera movement.
- A visually clear Hiroshima-to-Nagasaki transition.
- Historically grounded clothing, transportation and architecture.
- Restrained representation of the two explosions.
- A powerful, reflective ending.

Use original reconstruction or properly licensed archival material.

Clearly distinguish reconstruction from authentic archival footage.

Avoid generic mushroom-cloud montages, fabricated archival footage, graphic casualties or emotionally manipulative effects.

## 5. Narration and sound

The delivery should be compelling but respectful.

Avoid sensational voice acting, exaggerated impacts or triumphant music over mass suffering.

Write natural narration with a powerful hook, clear chronology and satisfying historical conclusion.
