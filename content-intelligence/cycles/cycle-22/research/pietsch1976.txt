Dimorphism, Parasitism and Sex: Reproductive Strategies
among Deepsea Ceratioid Anglerfishes
THEODORE W. PIETSCH
Knowledge of the reproductive biology of the Ceratioidei is reviewed
and new and scattered evidence is brought together in an attempt to
reevaluate some previously accepted ideas concerning the unique sexual
habits of these fishes. Notes on taxonomic content, available material,
occurrence of parasitism, gravid females and ripe males and the ability
of males to ingest food independently of the female are given for each
ceratioid family, after which this information is summarized and dis-
cussed. Data are presented to support at least three reproductive strategies
utilized by ceratioid anglerfishes: sexually mature males of the Ceratiidae,
Linophrynidae and perhaps the Neoceratiidae, are obligatory sexual
parasites; those of the Caulophrynidae and the oneirodid genus Lep-
tacanthichthys are probably facultative sexual parasites; and those of the
Melanocetidae, Himantolophidae, Gigantactinidae and at least some
oneirodid genera, are non-parasitic and perhaps spawn during a non-
parasitic, temporary attachment.
The four ceratioid families in which sexual parasitism has been pre-
viously recorded appear to form a highly specialized, monophyletic group,
most likely derived from some oneirodid-like ancestor. If a facultative
sexual parasitism exists for some ceratioids, particularly some oneirodid
genera, it would form a convenient evolutionary intermediate between
those ceratioids with obligatory sexual parasitism and those whose males
never become parasitic.
ERATIOID anglerfishes constitute a group
of 11 teleost families and nearly 100 spe-
cies that are distributed throughout the world
ocean below a depth of approximately 300
meters. They are most strikingly characterized
by having an extreme sexual dimorphism in
which males are dwarfed and, in some species,
become parasitically attached to the body of
a relatively gigantic female. The males of most
species are equipped with large nostrils, ap-
parently for homing in on a female-emitted,
species-specific pheromone; normal jaw teeth
are lost during metamorphosis, but are replaced
by a set of pincher-like denticles at the anterior
tips of the jaws for grasping and holding fast
to a prospective mate. In some forms, attach-
ment is followed by fusion of epidermal tissues
and, eventually, by a uniting of the circulatory
systems so that the male becomes dependent on
the female for blood-transported nutriment,
while the female becomes a kind of self-fertil-
izing hermaphroditic host (Bertelsen, 1951:251).
Since the time of its discovery, 50 years ago, the
story of sexual parasitism in ceratioid angler-
fishes has become a part of common scientific
knowledge. However, the known facts con-
cerning this remarkable reproductive strategy
have not been thoroughly and satisfactorily
analyzed, despite the elegant work of Bertelsen
(1951). In 1922, the Icelandic ichthyologist Bjarni
Saemundsson published a remarkable descrip-
tion of two small fish attached by their snouts
to the belly of a large female deepsea anglerfish
identified as Ceratias holboelli. Not recognizing
them as dwarf males, Saemundsson (1922) de-
scribed them as the young of the same species.
"I can form no idea of how, or when, the
larvae, or young, become attached to the mother;
I cannot believe that the male fastens the egg
to the female. This remains a puzzle for some
future researcher to solve" (Saemundsson, 1922,
translated from Danish in Regan, 1925b).
Three years later, Regan (1925b) dissected a
small fish attached to a newly discovered female
Ceratias holboelli and concluded that the small
fish must be a male, parasitic on the female.
The male fish is "merely an appendage of the
female, and entirely dependent on her for
nutrition, ... so perfect and complete is the
union of husband and wife that one may almost
be sure that their genital glands ripen simul-
taneously, and it is perhaps not too fanciful to
think that the female may possibly be able to

Following Regan's (1925b) initial discovery of
sexual parasitism, and his subsequent reports
of 1926, 1930 and 1932 (with E. Trewavas),
several authors, notably Parr (1930, 1932),
Waterman (1939) and particularly Bertelsen
(1951), further analyzed and discussed the prob-
lems of sexual parasitism, often supporting con-
flicting hypotheses. Interested readers are re-
ferred to the historical review of Bertelsen
(1951:244-250).
Bertelsen (1951:244) listed from the literature
20 examples of ceratioid females with parasiti-
cally attached males (Saemundsson, 1922, 1939;
Regan, 1925a, b, 1926, 1930; Parr, 1930; Regan
and Trewavas, 1932; Beebe, 1938; Barbour,
1941; Bertelsen, 1943; Bigelow and Barbour,
1944; Beebe and Crane, 1947). Since 1951, 18
additional examples have been reported, 13 of
which belong to the ceratiid genera, Ceratias
and Cryptopsaras (Abe and Nakamura, 1954;
Fast, 1957; Shoemaker, 1958; Okada, 1961;
Ueno, 1966; Abe, 1967; Penrith, 1967; Ueno
and Abe, 1967; Kobayashi et al., 1968; Jones,
1972; Fitch, 1973; Pietsch, 1975). In the
Linophrynidae, Kramp (1953) described an addi-
tional parasitized Borophryne apogon; the first
examples of parasitism in Linophryne lucifer,
L. algibarbata and L. coronata were reported by
J6nsson (1967), Behrmann (1974) and Bertelsen
(1976), respectively. Pietsch and Nafpaktitis
(1971) described an anomalous case of a sexually
mature male of Melanocetus johnsoni (Melano-
cetidae) attached, but without tissue fusion,
to an adolescent female Centrophryne spinulosa
(Centrophrynidae). Wickler (1961) described the structure and
function of the angling apparatus of ceratioids
and discussed some problems of pair formation.
Olsson (1974) recently reexamined histological
sections of an attached Edriolychnus schmidti
male described and figured by Regan and
Trewavas (1932, figs. 3-6), with respect to the
endocrine glands. Results showed that those
organs involved in regulation of various internal
environmental factors, as well as those involved
in adaption to external factors, appear poorly
developed or reduced, whereas mechanisms regu-
lating reproductive activities seem to be well
developed.
METHODS AND MATERIALS
In addition to reporting new examples of
attached males in collections around the world,
the purpose of this paper is to bring together
new and scattered bits of data that have accumu-
lated in recent years and to reevaluate, on the
basis of this new information, some previously
accepted ideas concerning ceratioid reproductive
biology. Notes on taxonomic content, available
material, occurrence of parasitism, gravid fe-
males and ripe males, and the ability of males
to ingest food independently of the female are
given below for each ceratioid family, after
which this information is summarized (Tables
1-3), discussed and conclusions reached. Stan-
dard lengths (SL) are used throughout. Defini-
tions of terms used for the different stages of
development follow Bertelsen (1951:10-11).
The developmental state of ceratioid gonads
has been subjected to little or no histological
verification. Sexually mature or gravid indi-
viduals are assumed to be those whose gonads
are obviously larger than those of other con-
specific individuals of a similar standard length.

FAMILY ACCOUNTS
Melanocetidae.-The Melanocetidae is now rep-
resented in collections by well over 400 meta-
morphosed females and approximately 75 free-
living, metamorphosed males belonging to one
genus and three, perhaps four, species. In all
this material, no parasitic male has ever been
found. The anomalous case of a Melanocetus
male attached to a female Centrophyrne spinu-
losa (Pietsch and Nafpaktitis, 1971; see Cen-
trophrynidae below) involved no fusion of male-
female tissue and is therefore not considered to
be a parasitic association.
Metamorphosed Melanocetus males with
large, ripe testes have been described (Parr,
1930, figs. 1-3; Regan and Trewavas, 1932, fig.
15; Bertelsen, 1951, fig. 15; Pietsch and Nafpak-
titis, 1971, fig. 4), but personal examination of
the ovaries of numerous specimens, including
the largest known individuals (up to 120 mm),
in the collections of the Institut fur Seefischerei,
Hamburg, failed to disclose any gravid female.
Metamorphosed males continue to feed after
metamorphosis: not only have food items been
found in the stomachs, but considerable growth
continues after stores in the liver have become
exhausted (Bertelsen, 1951:243). Of 20 meta-
morphosed males examined by me, seven had
copepods or other unidentifiable invertebrates
in their stomachs. This ability to take prey after
metamorphosis is reflected in the relatively
large, heavily toothed upper and lower dentic-
ular bones of adult Melanocetus males (Fig.
1A; also Regan and Trewavas, 1932, figs. 26,
27; Bertelsen, 1951:39, 250).
Two circular scars, each approximately 2 mm
in diameter similar to one found by Maul (1961,
fig. 15) on the holotype of Himantolophus
albinares, were observed on the belly of a large
female Melanocetus sp. (111.0 mm, LACM).
Clearing and staining of this scar tissue failed
to disclose embedded remains of denticular
bones that might have been left behind by a
once attached male (see Himantolophidae
below).
Himantolophidae.-The genus Himantolophus,
only known member of the Himantolophidae,
is known from approximately 50 adolescent and
adult females and perhaps a dozen metamor-
phosed males representing at least three spe-
cies. Parasitic males are unknown. Ovaries
containing ripe or nearly ripe eggs have been
found in a number of large females (400-580
mm); males with large testes have also been
described (Regan and Trewavas, 1932:21; Bertel-
sen, 1951, fig. 26; Mead et al., 1964). One

Diceratiidae.-The Diceratiidae, comprising two
monotypic genera, is poorly known: Diceratias
is represented in collections by 15 metamor-
phosed females (21.0-235.0 mm); Paroneirodes
by 21 metamorphosed females (19.0-178.0 mm).
A 200 mm Diceratias bispinosus in the collec-
tions of the Institute of Oceanology, Moscow,
has quite large ovaries (35% of SL) that con-
tain no developing eggs. Males are represented
by a single metamorphosed specimen (17.0 mm;
testes not examined) named Laevoceratias liparis
by Parr (1927), and questionably referred to
this family by Bertelsen (1951:70).
Oneirodidae.-The Oneirodidae, by far the
largest of the 11 recognized ceratioid families,
includes 14 genera and nearly 50 species, more
than half of all described ceratioids. A number
of the genera are well represented in collections,
whereas others are still known only from the
holotype (Table 1). Gravid females are known
in seven genera (Bertelsen, 1951:26, Mead et al.,
1964; Pietsch, 1972b, c, 1973, 1974); males with
large testes in three genera (Bertelsen 1951:26,
112, fig. 69; personal examination).
Previous to this report no evidence had in-
dicated sexual parasitism in the family Oneirodi-
dae. Food items have not been found in the
stomach of any oneirodid male (but few speci-
mens are available for examination). However,
the males of at least some genera grow con-
siderably after metamorphosis. Metamorphosis
stages of Oneirodes measure 6.0-9.5 mm,
whereas the largest known metamorphosed
specimen is 16.5 mm long (Pietsch, 1974). These
data indicate that the males probably continue
to feed after metamorphosis and are thus not
nutritionally dependent on a parasitic associ-
ation with the female; the especially well de-
veloped denticular apparatus of males of this
family has been thought to be used for tem-
porary attachment without subsequent tissue
fusion. Recently, however, a parasitically at-
tached male of the oneirodid Leptacanthichthys
gracilispinis (LACM 33625-2), the first to be
recorded for the family, was discovered among
collections made by the R/V VELERO IV of the
University of Southern California during the
1973 Costa Rica Expedition. The female (56.0
mm) is the largest of the 13 known specimens
of L. gracilispinis. The male is the first record

Centrophrynidae.-The monotypic Centro-
phrynidae, recently reviewed by Pietsch (1972a),
is now known from approximately 25 metamor-
phosed females (25.0-230.0 mm), none of which
is gravid. Males are represented by only two
specimens, both in metamorphosis stage (11.5-
16.0 mm), with quite small testes and little de-

Ceratiidae.-The Ceratiidae includes two gen-
era: Ceratias with one, perhaps two, species,
and Cryptopsaras with one. Both are among
the most commonly collected ceratioids, together
represented by well over 350 metamorphosed
females, 75 free-living males and 20 sexually
parasitized females. As pointed out by Bertelsen
(1951:245), all free-living Ceratias males are
small, less than 12.0 mm, whereas attached males
are considerably larger, 24.0-118.0 mm (Bigelow
and Barbour, 1944; Abe, 1967), having a weight
several times greater than that of the largest
free-living male. The size ranges of free-living
and attached Cryptopsaras males, contrasting
with those of Ceratias, overlap slightly; the
largest known free-living male measures 10.5
mm (14.3 mm total length; Bertelsen, 1951:144);
attached males range from 9.8 to 73.0 mm (see
below; Penrith, 1967). No free-living male with
large testes has ever been discovered, yet large,
ripe testes have been described in several at-
tached males (Regan, 1925a, b, fig. 4; Saemunds-
son, 1939; Bertelsen, 1943, 1951; Olsson, 1974).
All known gravid females (Bertelsen, 1943, 1951;
Fast, 1957; Shoemaker, 19'58; Mead et al.,
1964; Fitch, 1973) have a parasitic male at-
tached.
The jaw apparatus of metamorphosed ceratiid
males appears to be unsuited to serve in prey
capture (Fig. 1B) and the alimentary canal is
undeveloped (Bertelsen, 1951:131, 245, fig. 92).
Not one of the specimens examined by Bertelsen
(1951:245) has food in its stomach. The few
millimeters that they increase in length during
and partly after metamorphosis consists of a
stretching of the body which does not mean an

DISCUSSION
The results of this investigation support
Bertelsen's (1951:247) belief that the sexual
parasitic mode of reproduction is obligatory in
some ceratioids. Examination of numerous spec-
imens of the Ceratiidae and Linophrynidae
has shown that free-living males and non-para-
sitized females never have well developed
gonads. Males thus do not mature unless they
are in parasitic association with a female, and fe-
males probably never become gravid until stimu-
lated by the permanent parasitic attachment of
a male. The jaw apparatus of free-living males
of these families seems to be unsuited to serve
in prey capture and the alimentary canal is
undeveloped, indicating that the males do not
feed after metamorphosis and thus depend on
a parasitic association with a female for long-
term survival. These two lines of evidence sug-
gest that spawning and fertilization in members
of these families occurs only during a per-
manent parasitic association of male and female.
Although sufficient data are not available to
say with certainty, males of the Neoceratiidae
probably also become obligatory sexual para-
sites. Of the 11 known females of Neoceratias,
four carry a parasitic male and at least two of
these have ovaries containing numerous de-
veloping eggs; the seven non-parasitized females
all have small ovaries without visible eggs. The
denticular apparatus of Neoceratias males is
highly modified and appears essentially useless
in prey capture, indicating that, like ceratiid and
linophrynid males, these males depend on a
parasitic association with a female for postmeta-
morphic survival.
Bertelsen (1951:247) also presented consider-
able evidence to show that the males of some
ceratioid species never become parasitically at-
tached. The Melanocetidae, Himantolophidae,
Gigantactinidae, and several oneirodid genera
(Table 1), although now well represented in col-
lections, have yielded no evidence of male
parasitism. Except in the Melanocetidae, some
females of these groups have been found to con-
tain large ovaries, with eggs visible to the naked
eye, and in all these taxa, Melanocetidae in-
cluded, free-living males with large testes have
been reported. Either sex is thus able to attain
sexual maturity without the presence of the
other. Although food items have been found
in the stomachs of melanocetid males only,
these and males of the Himantolophidae, Gi-
gantactinidae, and the oneirodid genus
Oneirodes undergo a postmetamorphic increase
in length of 7 to 12 mm, indicating that they are
able to sustain themselves independent of the
female after energy stores in their liver have
been exhausted. That spawning takes place
during a temporary sexual attachment, not in-
volving fusion of male and female tissues, is
supported by the presence in these males of

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
1) Knowledge of ceratioid reproductive biology
is historically reviewed and brought up to
date.
2) Sexual parasitism is described in Lepta-
canthichthys gracilispinis, the first known
occurrence of parasitism in the Oneirodidae
and the first record of a male for the genus.
3) Extremely young sexually parasitized females
of Cryptopsaras couesi indicate that females
of this species are able to elicit a search
response in a conspecific male, as well as to
provide cues for specific identification by
the male at an early age.
4) There appears to be no mechanism that
prevents additional males from becoming at-
tached to a previously parasitized female.
5) Two specimens of Neoceratias spinifer have
been found to lack openings to the pharynx
leading to the gills and opercular openings,
which are present in the area of attachment
of all previously described examples of para-
sitically attached males. The gills of these
attached males, however, are as well de-
veloped as those of free-living males of other
ceratioid families, indicating that sufficient
oxygen is probably not available via the
blood of the female and that this gas is
extracted from water that is pumped in
and out through the opercular opening.
6) The sexual parasitic mode of reproduction
is obligatory in the Ceratiidae and Linophryi-
dae, and probably in the Neoceratiidae.
7) Males of the Melanocetidae, Himanto-
lophidae, Gigantactinidae and of several
oneirodid genera, probably never become
parasitic. Spawning and fertilization may
take place during a temporary sexual at-
tachment that does not involve fusion of
male and female tissues.
8) Sexual parasitism may be facultative in the
Caulophrynidae and in the oneirodid Lepta-
canthichthys.
9) The remaining ceratioid taxa are still so
poorly known that little can now be con-
cluded concerning their mode of reproduc-
tion.
