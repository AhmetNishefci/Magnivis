## Exact narration

Earth had nuclear reactors about two billion years ago. Nobody built them.

At Oklo, in what is now Gabon, uranium-rich rock sustained nuclear chain reactions.

Scientists found the clues in 1972: missing uranium-235, and the distinctive products of fission.

But radioactive rock alone is not a reactor. One splitting nucleus must release neutrons that can split more nuclei, keeping a chain going.

Back then, natural uranium contained more uranium-235, because that isotope decays faster than uranium-238.

Geology concentrated the uranium. The deposit had to retain enough neutrons, with few impurities that would absorb them.

Groundwater supplied another crucial ingredient.

Collisions with hydrogen in the water slowed the neutrons, making further uranium-235 fissions more likely. Enough kept the chain alive.

In one studied reaction zone, xenon isotope evidence points to repeated starts and stops.

Researchers infer that heating drove moderating water away, stopping the chain. Cooling let water return, and fission restart.

That cycle is reconstructed from traces in the rock, not an ancient clock anyone watched.

No machinery. No electricity. Just heat from nuclear fission, sustained by fuel, water, and geology, long before humans existed.
