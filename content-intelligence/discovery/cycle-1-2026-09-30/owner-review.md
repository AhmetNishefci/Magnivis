### 1. The traffic jam with nothing at the front

**Domain:** transportation, complex-systems

**One-sentence premise:** Small speed disturbances can grow into stop-and-go waves in sufficiently dense traffic even without a physical obstruction.

**Why someone stops scrolling:** A familiar frustration becomes a visible mystery: the jam moves backward while cars move forward.

**Possible hook direction — not final copy:** Begin on a jam and pull back to reveal an unobstructed road; ask what everyone is stopping for.

**What the viewer learns:** Congestion can be a collective instability, rather than evidence that something is blocking the road.

**Visual storytelling opportunity:** Overhead cars on a loop, a small spacing disturbance, then a backward-moving band of stopped cars; an original explanatory animation rather than copied experiment footage.

**Research / evidence outlook:** Sugiyama and colleagues’ 2008 controlled circular-road experiment is a direct primary lead. Locate the full paper before any numeric or experimental-detail claims.

**Main risk or uncertainty:** Do not claim every jam has this cause, blame one driver, prescribe driving behavior, or represent an illustrative simulation as measured data.

**Why it fits Magnivis:** Reveals an invisible everyday system with moving visual explanation and a satisfying causal payoff.

**Possible longer-term value:** Follow-ups on queue waves, traffic-flow control and bottlenecks; eventual hidden-systems long-form.

**Comment / share / save hypothesis:** Recognizable experience invites accounts of unexplained jams; sharing potential is a hypothesis, not measured demand.

**Proposed explanatory scope:** 30–40 seconds

**Freshness and duplicate check:** Engineering category overlaps Human Engineering; neither traffic waves nor collective instability appears in the six historical stories.

**Feasibility lead:** [Traffic jams without bottlenecks—experimental evidence for the physical mechanism of the formation of a jam](https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/93262/NewJPhys_10_033001.pdf). ### 2. The deaths that pointed to a water pump

**Domain:** history, public-health, epidemiology

**One-sentence premise:** During London’s 1854 cholera outbreak, Snow combined geographic patterns with water-use investigation to implicate the Broad Street pump.

**Why someone stops scrolling:** A street map turns into a detective story with a specific suspect: one ordinary water pump.

**Possible hook direction — not final copy:** Show deaths around several water sources, then ask why one pump kept appearing in the investigation.

**What the viewer learns:** Spatial patterns are clues; exposure histories and exceptions help test a causal explanation.

**Visual storytelling opportunity:** Original schematic Soho map, clustered marks, pump locations and traced water-use connections; avoid dramatic death imagery.

**Research / evidence outlook:** UCLA hosts Snow’s historical account and documentary material. Lightweight inspection already shows the epidemic was declining before handle removal.

**Main risk or uncertainty:** The map alone did not prove causation; avoid the lone-genius myth, “first disease map,” and “removing the handle instantly stopped cholera.” Chronology and Whitehead’s contribution need later review.

**Why it fits Magnivis:** A remarkable true story resolved by evidence, rather than an isolated history fact.

**Possible longer-term value:** Epidemiological detective stories, other water-supply comparisons and a deeper history of causal reasoning.

**Comment / share / save hypothesis:** Invites questions about what would count as evidence; an evidence-rich history story could earn saves.

**Proposed explanatory scope:** 40–50 seconds

**Freshness and duplicate check:** No subject or claim overlap with the six historical stories.

**Feasibility lead:** [Broad Street outbreak: handle removal and Reverend Whitehead](https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_e.html). Access on 2026-09-30: `page-excerpt`. ### 3. Why your friends can make everyone look more popular

**Domain:** sociology, network-science, statistics

**One-sentence premise:** People with many connections appear in more friendship lists, biasing the sample toward highly connected people.

**Why someone stops scrolling:** An emotionally familiar comparison has a surprisingly impersonal mathematical explanation.

**Possible hook direction — not final copy:** Introduce a tiny friendship network and ask why the same person keeps appearing in everyone’s list.

**What the viewer learns:** Sampling people through their connections differs from sampling people uniformly.

**Visual storytelling opportunity:** A handful of nodes, friendship edges, then duplicated appearances in sampled lists; make the weighting visible without a dense equation.

**Research / evidence outlook:** Feld’s 1991 original paper and a 2021 Journal of Complex Networks analysis are identified. Publisher abstract and later analysis are accessible; one repository PDF link failed.

**Main risk or uncertainty:** “Every friend is more popular than you” is false. Different averaging procedures, equal-degree networks and individual exceptions require careful wording; no claims about happiness or personal inadequacy.

**Why it fits Magnivis:** Turns a social experience into an understandable hidden structure without self-help or diagnostic language.

**Possible longer-term value:** Class-size paradox, biased online perceptions and network-based sampling; broader series on misleading averages.

**Comment / share / save hypothesis:** A personal but nonjudgmental premise may invite discussion; do not convert that into a guaranteed comment forecast.

**Proposed explanatory scope:** 35–45 seconds

**Freshness and duplicate check:** No subject or claim overlap with existing content.

**Feasibility lead:** [Why Your Friends Have More Friends Than You Do](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/229693). Access on 2026-09-30: `abstract-only`. ### 4. A majority can prefer A, then B, then C—and still loop

**Domain:** philosophy, social-choice, mathematics

**One-sentence premise:** Three internally consistent rankings can produce a pairwise majority cycle: A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A.

**Why someone stops scrolling:** A vote that seems to choose a winner instead forms a loop.

**Possible hook direction — not final copy:** Let three people rank three dinner choices, then reveal that each option beats one and loses to another.

**What the viewer learns:** Aggregating consistent individual preferences need not produce a consistent group ranking.

**Visual storytelling opportunity:** Three compact preference cards, successive two-option comparisons and a clean circular arrow; no party imagery.

**Research / evidence outlook:** Stanford’s Voting Methods entry explains Condorcet’s paradox. The eventual small worked example can be checked directly by enumeration.

**Main risk or uncertainty:** This is a mathematical possibility, not a claim that every election fails or that democracy is irrational. Keep the example small enough for mobile comprehension.

**Why it fits Magnivis:** A defensible paradox with a visual proof and a reframe about group decisions.

**Possible longer-term value:** Voting-rule comparisons, fairness tradeoffs, Arrow’s theorem with its assumptions and longer-form social choice.

**Comment / share / save hypothesis:** Viewer choice before the reveal may invite comments; avoid partisan bait.

**Proposed explanatory scope:** 35–45 seconds

**Freshness and duplicate check:** No subject overlap with existing stories.

**Feasibility lead:** [Voting Methods, section 3.1: Condorcet’s Paradox](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/voting-methods/). Access on 2026-09-30: `page-excerpt`. Institutional reference section located. A proof example and its assumptions still need full claim review; this is theory, not empirical election evidence. Source remains unreviewed; proposed factual wording remains unverified.

### 10. Why a forward-moving wheel runs backward on film

**Domain:** film-studies, signal-processing, physics

**One-sentence premise:** Discrete frames can sample repeating wheel spokes in positions that suggest reverse motion: temporal aliasing.

**Why someone stops scrolling:** The image contradicts the motion, and the viewer can see the illusion before hearing an explanation.

**Possible hook direction — not final copy:** Place a forward arrow beside a wheel that appears to turn backward; ask which one the camera is missing.

**What the viewer learns:** Video is sampled motion, and repeated patterns can hide what happened between frames.

**Visual storytelling opportunity:** Original rotating wheel, sparse frame snapshots and a marked-spoke teaching diagram; no copyrighted film clips needed.

**Research / evidence outlook:** MIT’s Foundations of Computer Vision explicitly identifies temporal aliasing as responsible for wheel reversal in movies; UC Davis provides a teaching demonstration.

**Main risk or uncertainty:** Scope to recorded or stroboscopic sampling, not a universal theory of vision. Demonstration requires controlled sampling and later transcoding checks; distinguish frame rate from shutter effects.

**Why it fits Magnivis:** Culture-adjacent everyday observation becomes a visual explanation of how images work.

**Possible longer-term value:** Moiré, rolling shutters, motion blur and a broader series on image-making illusions.

**Comment / share / save hypothesis:** The visible contradiction may prompt rewatches and examples; performance is unknown.

**Proposed explanatory scope:** 25–35 seconds

**Freshness and duplicate check:** Physics adjacency to Speed of Light, but no speed, distance or astronomy claim reuse.

**Feasibility lead:** [Image Sampling and Aliasing](https://visionbook.mit.edu/sampling_and_aliasing.html). Access on 2026-09-30: `page-excerpt`. Chapter excerpt on temporal aliasing accessed. Does not support a claim that human visual perception always operates like a film camera. Source remains unreviewed; proposed factual wording remains unverified.

**Why it fits Magnivis:** A large built system explained by a small, understandable mechanism.

**Possible longer-term value:** Lock water budgets, canal design tradeoffs, trade routes and infrastructure long-form.

**Comment / share / save hypothesis:** The uphill-ocean contradiction may support shares and explanatory questions; no guaranteed outcome.
